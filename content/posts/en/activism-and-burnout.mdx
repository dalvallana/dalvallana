Anyone who has spent time in animal advocacy knows the pattern. Someone discovers the scale of what happens to animals, feels the urgency of it in their whole body, and throws themselves into the work. For a few months they are everywhere: every outreach event, every meeting, every late-night message thread. Then, often without warning, they disappear.

Burnout is not a personal failing. It is a predictable outcome of treating a decades-long effort as a sprint. And it turns out that some of the lessons I learned the hard way on difficult engineering projects apply surprisingly well here.

## The urgency trap

The suffering is real and it is happening now. That makes it feel irresponsible to rest, to say no, or to work at a sustainable pace. Every hour not spent on the cause can feel like a betrayal of the animals it is meant to help.

But urgency is a poor planning tool. On software projects, the teams that treat every week as a crunch ship worse work, make more mistakes, and lose their best people. Movements are no different. An activist who stays for twenty years at a steady pace will accomplish far more than one who burns brightly for six months and then never comes back.

> Pace yourself not because the cause can wait, but because it cannot afford to lose you.

## Lessons from long projects

Some habits from engineering translate almost directly.

### Define what done looks like

Vague goals are exhausting because they never end. "End animal exploitation" is a mission, not a task. Breaking it down into concrete, finishable pieces, such as a campaign with a clear ask, a workshop with a set date, or a sanctuary fundraiser with a target, gives you moments of completion. Those moments matter more than they seem.

### Measure what you can

It is easy to feel like nothing changes. Keeping a simple record of what you did and what came of it helps counter that feeling with evidence. How many conversations, how many people signed up, how many restaurants added an option. The numbers will never capture the whole picture, but they are a useful antidote to despair.

### Rotate the hard work

On-call rotations exist because nobody can be the person who gets paged forever. In advocacy, the emotionally heaviest work, like investigations, footage review, or confrontational outreach, should be shared and rotated too. Nobody should carry it alone, and nobody should carry it indefinitely.

## Signs to watch for

Burnout rarely announces itself. It tends to arrive gradually, through small changes that are easy to dismiss:

- Feeling resentment toward people who are not doing as much
- Losing interest in things that used to bring joy outside the movement
- Sleeping badly, or sleeping too much
- Dreading meetings or events you once looked forward to
- A growing sense that nothing you do matters

None of these mean you should quit. They mean it is time to adjust the load before the load adjusts you.

## What sustainable looks like

Sustainable activism is not doing less out of indifference. It is choosing a pace you could keep for years, and protecting it.

For me, that has meant a few concrete commitments:

1. Keeping at least one day a week entirely free of movement work.
2. Saying no to new commitments until an old one ends.
3. Maintaining friendships and hobbies that have nothing to do with the cause.
4. Talking openly with other activists about how we are actually doing.
5. Remembering that rest is part of the work, not a break from it.

None of this is original, and none of it is easy. But each one has made it more likely that I will still be here, still doing this, a decade from now.

## The community matters

Individual habits only go so far. Movements that last build cultures where rest is normal, where people can step back without being judged, and where newcomers are welcomed without being immediately overloaded.

If you organize, it is worth asking whether your group makes it easy or hard to stay for the long haul. Are expectations clear? Is the emotionally difficult work shared? Do people feel safe saying they need a break? The answers shape who stays and who quietly drifts away.

## A closing thought

The animals need advocates who are angry, committed, and clear-eyed. They also need advocates who are still around in twenty years. Those two things are not in conflict. Taking care of yourself is how you make sure your commitment outlasts your first wave of urgency.

Go at a pace you can keep. The long game is the only one that wins.
