There was a time when I could sit calmly with family and friends at a table full of animal products and feel no conflict at all. Today I can't. For me, if I know there is exploitation behind what we are eating, sitting there and taking part in it means, in a way, normalizing it, and it makes me feel complicit.

There's an analogy that helps me explain it: if I had a friend who drugged women in order to abuse them, and I chose to go out partying with him knowing what he does, I would find it very hard to consider myself completely uninvolved, even if I never took part in the abuse myself. If I'm aware of what is happening and decide to stay there as if nothing were wrong, there is a moral responsibility, and you could say I'm complicit. I feel something similar about animal exploitation.

## Why I no longer ask people to cut down

The way I do activism has changed too. I used to recommend that people reduce how many animal products they consumed. Today I'm no longer comfortable doing that. I fully understand that if I get someone to go from eating meat seven days a week to two, the result can be positive for the animals. But for me, the problem lies in the message itself. If I had a friend who hit his partner every day, I could tell him to try not to hit her on Mondays and Wednesdays. The outcome would objectively be better than if he kept hitting her every day, but I think it would be morally questionable for that to be my advice. What I should ask him is to stop hitting her. That doesn't mean I condemn those who use consumption-reduction or harm-reduction strategies; that approach just isn't for me anymore.

## Welfarism: not everything helps

And this is where welfarism comes in. I know there are very different strategies within the movement, such as welfarism and abolitionism. Personally, I'm much closer to abolitionism, what some call “purist veganism”, but I don't think it makes sense to wage war on those who think differently. In fact, I'm increasingly convinced that we need different kinds of activism. The diversity of people and of ways of seeing the world is far too great to expect a single message to work for all of them.

That said, I think we have to be responsible and careful with our strategies, because it isn't always true that “every little bit helps”. One of the criticisms of welfarism is precisely that certain reforms can become a way to soothe consciences and end up encouraging consumption. For example, if we manage to end the force-feeding of ducks for _foie gras_, some people might come to see the product as less cruel, feel less guilty eating it and end up buying more of it. That effect is very hard to quantify, but I think the risk is real and we need to take it into account.

And, paradoxically, I myself have campaigned to end cages in the egg industry and to end force-feeding. It's not that I'm proud of this contradiction, but I don't feel guilty about it either. I don't think everything has to be black and white. If I were a duck condemned to die, I would rather not be locked in a cage or tortured by force-feeding during the short and miserable life I had left. I can stand for the end of exploitation and, at the same time, want to reduce the suffering of those trapped in it.

## Against the “vegan wars”

What I would like to avoid at all costs is what I call the “vegan wars”: the infighting among vegans over who is right. I think we can hold very different positions, argue for them with total conviction and, at the same time, understand that we are on the same side of something fundamental: challenging a system of exploitation that harms animals.

## A simple test: think of humans

When I'm unsure, a very simple test helps me a lot: imagine the same situation with human beings. For example, in the past, if I was given a basket full of animal products at work, I would simply pass it on to someone who ate them. But I ask myself: if we were given human flesh, would we calmly hand it over to our cannibal friends or relatives? Or would it seem more appropriate to refuse it and show respect for the person who was killed, perhaps by burying them? If our answer changes only because the victim belongs to another species, I think that's something important we need to question. That's why I'm no longer comfortable giving those baskets to anyone. If there's still time, I'd rather ask people not to spend money on that gift, and I suggest that gifts be free of animal exploitation.

## A matter of justice

Of course, each person can walk this path differently and set their own limits. But for me there is one thing that isn't a personal preference or simply a lifestyle: veganism is a matter of justice. And, from my perspective, justice for animals isn't one option among many, but a _moral imperative_.

That's why, even though I deeply respect that everyone is at a different point, I can no longer relate to certain situations the way I used to.

In the end, I think we vegans need spaces to question ourselves, listen to each other, confront our own contradictions and learn every day how to do better in our fight for animal liberation 🌱🐷.
